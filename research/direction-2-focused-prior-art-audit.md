# Direction 2: focused prior-art audit

**Date:** 1 November 2026
**Decision: REFORMULATE**

## 1. Scope and decision

The question investigated is: for a coupled thermomechanical continuum, when do specified mechanical and calorimetric observations determine enough of the material's internal state to uniquely determine its thermodynamic storage and future initial-boundary evolution? This is a question, not an established gap. No candidate theory or demonstrated novelty has been found. Direction 1 remains **REJECTED AS A NEW FOUNDATIONAL CONTINUUM THEORY**.

The generic state/work/energy question is substantially anticipated, including in non-isothermal memory. In particular, Fabrizio, Gentili and Golden (2004), §3, explicitly prove a response/work equivalence under their assumptions and represent minimum free energy on minimal states. Their independent variables include deformation and temperature history, and their generalized response includes stress and internal energy. Carillo compares heat-memory and viscoelastic equivalences. Holicki et al. explicitly distinguish input-output minimality from energy matching. The 2012 Amendola–Fabrizio–Golden book advertises thermoelastic memory controllability and inverse problems as well as minimal states. Adding heat, energy, a quotient, or a PDE to the broad question is not, by itself, an identified contribution.

Nevertheless, these sources do not establish that **every** coupled continuum inverse problem with prescribed finite boundary ports, actual heat-flow measurements and unknown initial memory has been solved. The broad question is underspecified; selected foundational sources were inaccessible in full; and observability, storage and evolution are different properties. Therefore a universal closure claim would exceed the evidence. The appropriate recommendation is to reformulate around a concrete class and experiment, and first verify overlap, not to announce a theorem, framework, or publication. A mathematical result, even if eventually new, would not itself establish a new foundational continuum theory.

## 3. Strongest close prior art: the non-isothermal case

Fabrizio–Gentili–Golden use the current deformation gradient and coldness (inverse absolute temperature) and their past histories as independent variables, in a continuum material-point setting. Their generalized response contains coldness-weighted Piola stress and specific internal energy. Their scaled free energy is Helmholtz free energy divided by temperature, **not** simply Helmholtz free energy. They assume, among other things, that this free energy and its rate do not depend on the coldness gradient, and take a Fourier-type heat-flux law. The paper reduces the local inequality to a generalized work form; its process is the rate of the independent field, and its admissible process set is closed under restriction and concatenation.

In §3 they define equivalent states by equal current independent fields and identical generalized response for every process. Proposition 3.1 states equality of work for every process follows, with the converse when the present fields agree, within their abstract process setting. Under their strong dissipation principle, a zero state and normalization, Theorem 3.2 states that minus the infimum of continuation work is the minimum free energy; the paper explains why it depends only on the minimal state. The theorem's proof is cited to earlier work, not reproduced there. This is much closer to the proposed idea than merely analogy: state, stress, internal energy, work, non-isothermal memory and a minimum storage are already connected.

However, generalized pointwise internal energy response is not identical to a realistic finite set of boundary calorimeter readings; all admissible pointwise continuations are not the same as spatially constrained actuators. The minimum of a specified class of scaled free energies is not uniqueness of every admissible physical storage, and a local material result is not an IBVP observability theorem. The paper itself notes that maximum free energy can depend on the adopted state definition unless restrictions are imposed. Its linear model assumes temperature variations small enough to neglect temperature-dependent time scales. No general identification result follows for arbitrary nonlinear memory or unknown spatially varying initial histories.

## 4. Mathematical distinctions and direct checks

Take a deterministic model with initial state x, a fixed set U of common admissible future protocols, and output trace Y(x,u) on the prescribed horizon. There must be a well-defined shared protocol domain: state-dependent feasibility requires separate treatment. Define observation equivalence as equality of these traces for every u in U. Histories may be equivalent under a material response map yet remain different functions; the corresponding **minimal state** is an equivalence class relative to the specified responses and admissible continuations, not necessarily an observable coordinate or a unique physical microstate. Enlarging outputs or protocols can refine the classes. A single finite experiment is weaker than equality under every admissible continuation.

Define work equivalence by equality of W(x,u) for each common u, not merely equality of an unordered set of work values. Thermodynamic equivalence needs a separate declared meaning: e.g., equality of specified internal energy, entropy, free energy and their relevant future traces after fixing gauges and constitutive conventions. Nothing makes this automatically the same relation as mechanical output equivalence. Storage identification asks which function(s) satisfy a supply inequality or match an actual measured physical energy; initial-state observability asks whether outputs identify x; evolution uniqueness asks whether a well-posed model plus adequate initial and boundary data determines the intended fields. Each has different quantifiers.

**Elementary implication, not a claimed new theorem.** Suppose the supply rate is a known function of the same protocol and observed output, is integrable, and W(x,u) is its time integral. Observation equivalence then implies equal W for each u. Thus the available storage

\[ A(x)=\sup_{u\in U}[-W(x,u)] \]

is identical for equivalent states if the identical protocol family and horizon convention are used; finiteness requires extra assumptions. This is an operational identity, not identification of an arbitrary physical Helmholtz energy. Reverse inference from equal work to equal output is not automatic: rich enough test inputs, localization, regularity and a nondegenerate supply pairing may be needed. FGG Proposition 3.1 supplies such a converse in its own abstract setting; Carillo gives a thermal-model equivalence. Neither licenses a converse for arbitrary sensor-limited experiments.

**Observable state does not make dissipative storage unique.** Consider the known scalar system \(\dot x=-x+u\), \(y=x+u\), with unrestricted real inputs and supply \(w=uy\). Here x is exactly observable from u,y. Both \(S_p(x)=p x^2/2\), for p=1 and p=1/2, are nonnegative and satisfy \(\dot S_p\le uy\): the difference is \(p x^2+(1-p)xu+u^2\), nonnegative for both choices. They give different values at any nonzero x despite identical dynamics, measured ports and a fixed zero at x=0. This is a systems-theory counterexample, not by itself a thermomechanical constitutive model; calibrated physical calorimetry or a separately specified storage selection can eliminate some alternatives. A known functional S combined with an observable state determines S(x), but does not identify S across unknown constitutive classes. Holicki et al. provide a substantially closer existing energy-matching example.

**Incomplete observation does not determine the full state.** For the passive system \(\dot x_1=-x_1+u,\ \dot x_2=-x_2,\ y=x_1\), initial states with equal x1 but distinct x2 have identical mechanical input-output traces for all u, while the internal quadratic \((x_1^2+x_2^2)/2\) differs. The hidden coordinate is not driven by u and is not observed. If its decay heat is actually measured in a specified thermal balance, those states can become distinguishable; the example does not prove failure of such calorimetry. It also does not establish a genuinely coupled continuum counterexample.

**Boundary outputs need not specify all future fields.** Equal outputs for all admitted controls ensure only equal outputs by definition. To identify the full spatial initial state one needs injectivity of the relevant observation map on the admissible state space; to predict the full future field one also needs a known well-posed evolution and known future boundary/body data. If only the observed output is sought, its equality is already built into the equivalence definition. No continuity or stability of inversion follows from injectivity alone.

## 5. Energy balance versus calorimetry and entropy

For a closed material body with all mechanical boundary power, body-force work, boundary heat flux and volumetric heat sources accounted for, the integrated first law determines the **change** in total internal plus kinetic energy from net supplied work and heat. Kinetic energy can be separated only when the required velocity/density information is known. Incomplete boundary coverage, unmeasured sources or heat losses invalidate even this inference. Absolute initial energy has a reference ambiguity, and a single integrated balance does not reconstruct a local energy density or a field of hidden states.

Helmholtz free energy is \(\psi=e-\theta\eta\); heat transfer is energy transfer, not in general entropy transfer times a single system temperature. The entropy inequality contains heat flux divided by local absolute temperature, body heat supply divided by temperature, and nonnegative internal production. Boundary heat alone, without temperature distributions, entropy calibration and constitutive/evolution assumptions, generally does not determine entropy or entropy production. A calorimeter may measure net heat, heat capacity or inferred heat source depending on the protocol; these are not interchangeable. Relative energy changes may constrain hidden storage, but neither a normalized energy level nor a unique admissible free-energy functional follows merely from the first law. FGG's special scaled free-energy/work construction shows how additional assumptions can make a stronger inference, and why the exact thermodynamic potential matters.

Charlès–Le Cam's heat-source reconstruction assumes known thermophysical parameters and a chosen constitutive model, and includes thermomechanical coupling in the interpretation of measured fields. Spencer et al. derive mechanical stress and entropy from the same chosen free energy and solve a coupled calibration problem. Neither amounts to measuring a universal hidden energy directly. Likewise the thermal memory papers show that a flux history can be part of a minimal-state analysis, not that arbitrary finite calorimeter ports determine a body-wide state.

## 6. Skeptical gap test and recommendation

**Evidence against a broad gap:** response/work equivalence and minimal free-energy representation in an explicitly non-isothermal history model (2004); rigid thermal memory equivalence and work (2005); multifield and nonlinear-memory minimal-state literature; abstract dissipativity and storage bounds (Willems); direct energy-versus-input-output counterexample (Holicki et al.); kernel identification and coupled thermomechanical calibration. A generic assertion that mechanical plus thermal data make a new state theory is untenable.

**What remains genuinely undecided:** whether a *specific* bounded-domain, coupled thermomechanical system with a *specified* memory kernel class, boundary actuator/sensor geometry, finite measurement horizon, available heat-flux resolution, unknown initial state and normalized physical energy admits a provably injective observation map, stable recovery, or a counterexample. This is an untested possibility, not evidence of originality. The book's inverse and controllability chapters and the inaccessible foundational papers could already cover decisive special cases. FGG's local generalized response contains internal energy, but its assumed all-process access does not settle finite-port calorimetry. Conversely, the inability to access papers is not evidence of a gap.

Before any further research authorization, define: (1) body, dimension, regularity and boundary split; (2) exact mechanical and thermal constitutive laws, coupling, kernel and unknown parameter/history classes; (3) initial compatibility and known reference state; (4) actuator inputs and a time horizon; (5) whether sensors observe local or integrated heat flux, temperature, displacement, traction and their precision; (6) the targeted object—physical internal energy, normalized Helmholtz energy, minimum available storage, or future field; (7) well-posedness and identifiability modulo explicit gauges; (8) distinction between uniqueness and stability. Then obtain the missing original papers and 2012 inverse/controllability chapters and search adjacent inverse-boundary and observability literature again. Any focused conjecture should be withheld until such a comparison identifies a real nontrivial residual problem. No numerical simulations, new model, manuscript or publication claim are justified by this audit.
